DNS records

DNS tells the world which servers speak for your domain. A few small records there decide whether your email is trusted.

The address book of the internet

Every domain has a list of small text entries called records. Other servers read them to learn where to deliver your mail and who may send it. You never see them in daily life, but they are the reason a message from your shop is believed or sent to spam.

Every record has three parts

  • Type: what kind of information it holds. We use TXT (a piece of text) and MX (where mail is delivered).
  • Name: which part of your domain it belongs to. @ means the domain itself, and a word such as _dmarc means a special place under it.
  • Value: the content, usually a long line of letters. Copy it exactly, without adding spaces.

The records Geserd asks you to add

DNS
Type  Name                  Value
TXT   <selector>._domainkey  v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBg...
TXT   @                     v=spf1 ip4:<server address> ~all
TXT   _dmarc                v=DMARC1; p=none
MX    @                     <mail server>   priority 10

The first three are about trust and the last one is about receiving. The dashboard prints the real values for your domain, so you never type them from memory: you copy them from the screen.

Where to add them

Open the DNS settings at the company where you bought the domain, create each record as shown, save, and press verify in the dashboard. Changes usually appear within minutes. The next guide shows each click.
